**What the numbers say.** The two pulses are identical to the oracle cycle for
cycle on all three songs but one note: every edge exact, none unmatched, every
run aligned at a shift of zero, and that includes the smooth vibrato's sweep
sequences, dozens per song. So are the sweep-unit mute and its `$4001 = $08`
cure, the `$4003` phase restart, and the downward sweep on both pulses,
including pulse 1's extra minus one. The one note is pulse 2's on the e2e
song, class 5 below. What differs falls into five classes, and none of them is
a chipvoice bug against nesdev; each is a convention of a 2005 oracle that the
documentation has since moved past.

1. **Frame timing.** The oracle's frame steps land two cycles after nesdev's
   7457, 14913, 22371, 29829, which `test/clock.mjs` pins. Anything the frame
   counter drives - an envelope step, a sweep step, a length or linear counter
   running out - happens two cycles later in the oracle, and a pulse whose
   period the sweep changed keeps a phase offset from then on. That is
   `script-envelope`, `script-sweep-up`, the pulse lines of `script-five-step`
   and the tail of `script-length`. Settled: blargg's own test ROMs, run below,
   check the frame timing to the cycle and pass against this core, so the
   hardware's timing is nesdev's and the 2005 oracle is the one that is off.
2. **The triangle's sequencer position.** The oracle powers its triangle on
   at 0; the hardware powers on at 15, and so does this core, since blargg's
   mixer test proved it (below). So the two differ on every silent cycle from
   the first, which is why every triangle line reads close to nothing
   identical. And when its counters reload, the oracle steps at once where the
   hardware waits for the timer's next expiry, so from the first note on the
   two sequencers are a couple of steps apart for good. The runs column is the
   number to read: the harness puts the sequence's hidden steps back and finds
   the oracle stepping on the same cycles, under a shift of at most a period.
3. **Envelope at power-on.** The oracle's `reset()` writes `$4003` to every
   voice, which leaves its envelope start flag set; its first frame clock then
   loads every decay level with 15. A voice put in envelope mode before that has
   decayed sounds at 15 in the oracle and at 0 here, where nesdev says the decay
   level is 0 until a `$4003` write is processed. The scripts start a tenth of a
   second in so that this does not colour them.

4. **The DMC's first cycle.** Its output unit starts silent with eight bits
   to go in ours, as nesdev describes it and Mesen does it, and with one in the
   oracle, so the first sample byte plays one bit period apart: every step of
   `script-dmc` lands 54 cycles later in the oracle, with the same values. And
   a `$4011` write in the oracle adjusts the amplitude through a DAC table, for
   the sound of the pop rather than the register's value, so the levels after
   one are the oracle's own. The steps still align.
5. **The forced clock.** A `$4017` write with bit 7 set clocks the sequencer
   at once: three or four cycles after the write on the hardware, which
   `apu_reset/4017_timing` checks and the chip passes, and zero or one in the
   oracle. The driver's smooth vibrato forces a clock on every period crossing,
   and the period it sets takes effect at the pulse's next timer reload; when
   that reload falls inside the three cycles the two disagree on, the oracle's
   edges sit a few cycles from ours for the rest of the note. One note of one
   song so far, pulse 2 of `song-e2e`; every other crossing is identical.

The noise is not compared: the oracle's register starts elsewhere, runs with the
inverse polarity and is not clocked exactly while muted, so its bit pattern
cannot match anything. Its rates and sequence lengths are pinned by the formula
tests, and its envelope shares the pulses' code.

**Does the envelope match?** Yes. Every pulse and noise envelope line reads
99.1-99.9 % identical, and what is left is not the envelope. The noise line's
shortfall is the pulses' own class-1 one-cycle offset, all 6716 of its
near-edges lining up under a shift of one. The pulses' residual - the
unmatched edges on `script-envelope` and `script-five-step`, each right after
a `$4003`/`$4007` write or a quarter frame - is *when* Mesen shows a volume,
not which volume. Its square channel recomputes its output on a register
write and on a timer reload, and nowhere else: `SquareChannel::TickEnvelope()`
and `TickLengthCounter()` (vendored, unchanged) move the envelope and the
length counter without touching the output, so a decay step, an envelope
restart or a length expiry reaches Mesen's trace at the pulse's next reload,
up to one timer period (2(P+1) cycles) late. The hardware has nothing to wait
for: nesdev's pulse page gates the envelope's volume straight through to the
mixer. Every one of those intervals closes within one timer period, and a
scratch build of the oracle that refreshes the output in both tick functions
removes all of them, on both scripts (P2-1; not committed, since decision 41
keeps the vendored code as it is). The decay level, the loop flag, the
constant-volume bit and the start flag's reload on the next quarter frame all
match: `ApuEnvelope::TickEnvelope`'s start-flag branch sets the counter to 15
and the divider to the volume register the instant it is next clocked,
exactly as nesdev's envelope page describes it and as this core does it.

**Does the sweep match, including muting and the pulse 1 / pulse 2 negate
difference?** Yes. The target-period arithmetic does on every write in the
corpus: the mute below period 8, the mute when the target overflows past
`$7FF`, and pulse 1's extra minus one on a negative sweep (which pulse 2 does
not apply). So does the divider, once the channel's sweep has been written
once - and that "once" is the whole of the two sweep scripts' shortfall
(`script-sweep-up` reads 87.71 % on pulse 2 in the block above, with almost
no edge lining up under any shift, where Nes_Snd_Emu reads 99.88 % under a
constant shift of two). Nesdev's sweep page counts a divider from P down to
0 and checks it before reloading or decrementing it, which this core's
`clockSweep()` (`packages/chipvoice/src/chips/nes/dsp.ts`) follows verbatim.
Mesen's `SquareChannel::TickSweep()` counts from P+1 down to 1, decrementing
first and checking the result: the same unit shifted by one, a divider of d
in nesdev's reading being d+1 in Mesen's, and the two fire, reload and honour
the reload flag on the same half-frame clocks. Except from power-on:
`Reset()` sets Mesen's divider *and* its period to 0, outside its own 1 to
P+1 range, so its first decrement wraps the unsigned byte to 255 and the
divider counts down from there until the channel's first `$4001`/`$4005`
write reloads it on the half-frame clock after. `script-sweep-up` writes
pulse 2's sweep as `$9B` (a P+1 of 2) eleven half-frame clocks after
power-on: this core's divider is at 0 and fires on the next clock; Mesen's
has only reached 245, spends that clock reloading, and fires two clocks
later, a full 4-step sequence (29830 cycles) late, after which every duty
edge of the note lands elsewhere. Pulse 1's sweep in the same script (a P+1
of 1) is one clock late, and `script-sweep-down` is the same cold start at
its own rate. A scratch build of the oracle with both reset to 1 (nesdev's
0) matches this core on every edge of both scripts (P2-1). Nesdev does not
give the divider's power-on value, so this is not evidence against Mesen's
algorithm, only against its start state, and it never reaches a song: the
driver writes `$4001 = $08` at every note start, and one half-frame clock
after that write Mesen's divider is back in range for good.

**What is left on the songs' pulses?** One write, twice. A period write that
lands on the very cycle a pulse's timer reloads is seen by that reload here,
as it is by Nes_Snd_Emu - the harness's convention is that a write lands
before the cycle it is stamped with is clocked - while Mesen's catch-up
(`WriteRam` runs the channels through the write's own cycle first) reloads
with the old period. The two then keep a constant phase offset of twice the
period difference until the song ends: 168 cycles on `song-studio`'s pulse 2,
whose `$4006` moved from `$AB` to `$FF` on a reload cycle during a smooth
vibrato, and 2 cycles on `song-golden`'s pulse 1, whose `$4002` moved from
`$E0` to `$E1`. A scratch copy of this core that reloads first, as Mesen
does, loses the offset on `song-studio`'s pulse 2. Nesdev and the test ROMs
say nothing about which half of a CPU cycle a timer reloads in relative to a
write, and the shim cannot adopt this core's order without losing the rest:
Mesen's pulse timer counts 2P+1 CPU cycles from wherever its first period
write left it rather than on a fixed APU-cycle parity, and on this corpus
its phase lines up with this core's precisely because it reloads before the
write. Kept as a difference of convention; what it changes is the phase of
a sustained note, which nobody hears. A second cause, the `$4017` forced
clock of `song-e2e`'s pulse 2 landing two cycles from this core's, was the
shim's own: see the oracle's README on its cycle count, fixed in P2-1, and
the block above now reads that pulse at 100 %.

**Does the triangle's start and phase match?** The phase matches once the
triangle is running: `script-linear`'s and `script-length`'s tails read
96.7 %, `script-five-step`'s 96.6 %, and every one of those shortfalls is
edges lining up exactly under the pulses' own class-1 one-cycle shift (868 of
868, 1396 of 1396, 440 of 440 respectively) - Mesen's sequencer steps on the
same cycles as this core's once both are running. The very first cycle does
not: Mesen's `ApuTimer` starts every channel's last-output at 0 and the
triangle never writes an initial value to it before the first timer fire, so
Mesen's triangle reads 0 from power-on until its first step, where nesdev and
blargg's `apu_mixer` test (which this core's power-on state cites already, see
"Power-on state" above) say the real hardware and this core both read 15.
Nes_Snd_Emu's triangle also starts at 0. Both oracles disagree with nesdev and
blargg's own recorded evidence here, in the same direction, so this is a known
limit of both oracles, not a finding against this core; see the oracle's
README. It is also why every triangle line in the table above reads 0.0000 %:
the scripts that never write the triangle's registers hold it silent the
whole time, so the two sides' cycle-zero disagreement never resolves.

**Does the noise's bit pattern match?** Yes, and this is what Mesen settles
that Nes_Snd_Emu cannot: the LFSR's power-on value (1), its feedback tap (bit
1 in mode 0, bit 6 in mode 1), its polarity (bit 0 set silences the channel)
and its clocking while muted all match, on every script and song in the
corpus, once the pulses' own class-1 one-cycle shift is allowed for (every
noise line's near-edges line up under exactly that shift - 18083 of 18083 on
`script-noise`, 12119 of 12119 on `script-length`). The songs' few unmatched
noise edges beyond that shift (270 of some 127000 on `song-e2e`) are the
output refresh the envelope paragraph describes, one step further: Mesen's
noise recomputes its output only on a timer tick, not on a register write
(`NoiseChannel::WriteRam` has no call that would) nor on an envelope or
length clock, so a new volume or a note start shows up to one noise period
late. Every such interval closes within one noise period, and the same
scratch refresh build removes all of them. Nes_Snd_Emu's noise starts
elsewhere and runs inverted, which is why the first oracle's block above does
not compare it at all.

**A note on the DMC's numbers.** `script-dmc` reads only 51.95 % against
Mesen, but every one of its steps carries the same value on both sides, 54
cycles apart, the identical offset and magnitude class 4 above already
documents for Nes_Snd_Emu. Neither oracle simulates the CPU stall a real DMA
read causes (the ticket that added this oracle asked for exactly that
simplification, since the stall does not change the APU's own timing in a
log-driven run - see the oracle's README), and both land on the same 54-cycle
constant as a result. Every other log's DMC line reads 100 %, since only
`script-dmc` restarts the channel from a cold buffer where this offset shows.

## Real game music

Digital parity above compares synthetic scripts and this project's own songs
against an oracle's *DSP output*. This section compares a different thing:
real NSFs' own *command stream*, extracted by running each file's own INIT
and PLAY routines on an offline 6502 (`scores/capture-nsf.mjs`, not
`packages/chipvoice`) and checked command-by-command against an independent
NSF player, [Game_Music_Emu](https://github.com/libgme/game-music-emu)
(pinned, `scores/arrangements/native-oracle.py`). Mario's Ground Theme and
Zelda's Overworld theme already prove this to an exact cycle
(`scores/arrangements/compare-native.mjs`, decision 29); the corpus below
grows that from one song per game to many independent NSFs, across several
sound drivers, each scored rather than asserted.

Every file is a homebrew or demo NSF whose licence explicitly allows
redistribution (CC0, CC-BY, public domain, or similarly permissive), with its
source URL, licence, author and SHA-256 recorded in

## VGM import

`importVgm` (`packages/chipvoice/src/vgm-import.ts`) reads a VGM 1.61+ file
whose header declares the NES APU's clock at offset `0x84`: register writes
(command `0xB4`) and DPCM sample data (data-block type `0xC2`, "NES APU RAM

APU, another chip's clock in the same header, and VGM versions outside
1.50-1.71: see the package's README and [CONFORMANCE.md](../CONFORMANCE.md).

## NSF export

The other direction: `exportNsf` (`packages/chipvoice/src/nsf.ts`) turns a
2A03 capture - `recordSong`'s or `planPerformance`'s own `events`/`cycles` -
into a standard NSF v1 file that plays in any NSF player, on real hardware or
in an emulator. NSF2/NSFe are not used: nothing here needs a feature either
adds (one song, ASCII metadata, no non-returning INIT) over v1's plainer
header.

The file carries its own tiny 6502 player, hand-assembled in
`packages/chipvoice/src/nsf.ts` from a small in-file mnemonic assembler
(`Asm6502`) rather than a build tool - the bytes it emits are exactly what
that source says, reproducible by anyone who reads it, never an opaque blob.
INIT sets up the APU once; PLAY runs once per 60 Hz NTSC frame and replays
that frame's writes from a compact per-frame encoding (a write-list, or a
run-length token for a stretch of silent frames, the common case since most
of a song is held notes) stored in bank-switched ROM, looping at the
capture's own loop point forever. A song longer than one 32 KB bank uses
more: the player keeps a single fixed 4 KB data window (`$9000`) and swaps
which ROM page is mapped there as it reads past it, rather than juggling
several windows.

Write timing is quantized to the frame: a capture stamps every write in CPU
cycles, but PLAY can only place writes at its own call, once per frame, so a
write lands at the start of the frame it falls in rather than at its real
cycle - at most one frame (~16.7 ms) early - and a burst of writes a source
driver spaced a few cycles apart (the 2A03 driver's own smooth-vibrato
`$4017` sequence among them - see below) collapses to however far apart
PLAY's own instructions land them. So is a capture with more writes in one
frame than the encoding's one-byte pair count can address (254), a loop
point outside the capture, and non-ASCII or over-length title/author/
copyright text - rejected loudly, never silently dropped.

DMC/DPCM sample playback is autonomous hardware DMA: the 2A03 itself, not
PLAY's own code, steals CPU cycles to read cartridge memory at `$C000-
$FFFF` while the CPU stalls, driven purely by `$4012`/`$4013`/`$4015` bit 4.
None of that needs a mid-frame instruction from PLAY, so a capture that
carries its sample memory (`NsfOptions.memory`, the same shape
`PerformancePlan.memory` already uses) exports normally: the sample bytes
are placed at their real addresses in a fixed upper bank (bank registers
4-7, `$C000-$FFFF`) that is never bank-switched away, so a real DMA read
always finds the right bytes no matter when it fires, and the
`$4010`-`$4013`/`$4015` writes replay at frame start like any other
register write. A capture that enables DMC without carrying its sample
bytes is rejected by name (`dmc_sample_missing`) rather than exported
silently short a sample. The one DMC-adjacent case this player genuinely
cannot carry is raw `$4011` streaming - writing the direct-load DAC many
times within a single frame to reconstruct a waveform without ever touching
the DMA channel, which needs each write timed to a fraction of a frame - and
that is rejected as `dmc_unsupported`, named with how many writes landed in
one frame and the limit. A single, occasional `$4011` write (a manual
percussion poke that never touches the DMA-driven side) is not streaming by
this definition and carries like any other register write.

Proven four ways, all against the same pinned Game_Music_Emu oracle
`nsf-corpus` uses (its build is reused; one CI cache step covers both
`nsf-corpus:check` and `nsf-export:check`):

1. Command stream (gates CI, exact): the export, replayed by GME, must
   reproduce the exact write stream this project's own offline 6502
   (`scores/capture-nsf.mjs`) gets from replaying the very same export -
   scored per file like `nsf-corpus`, using its own `compareNsfTrace`, and
   required to match exactly (`matched === total`, not just "matched some").
   Both sides run identical bytes, so anything short of an exact match means
   the player program is wrong, not that quantization lost something. This
   checks the export against itself; it says nothing on its own about
   whether the export reproduces the source - proof #2 does.
2. Frame writes (gates CI, exact, deterministic, not audio): the source
   capture's own register writes and GME's trace of the export, each
   bucketed into 60 Hz frames (`Math.floor(cycle / PERIOD)`), must list the
   exact same writes - address, value, order - frame for frame, after one
   constant frame offset (found by a small search, the same one proof #3
   uses), again required to match exactly. Two independently INIT'd NSF
   players never agree on which frame index is "PLAY call zero" (every
   player's own INIT-to-first-PLAY overhead differs - GME's own reset
   ceremony alone consumes a full frame before it ever calls the exported
   player's PLAY routine), so a constant offset is expected and searched
   for, not assumed to be zero.

   A source frame is only required to match while its own real-time
   position (`sourceFrame + offset`) still falls inside the export's
   one-shot pass through the content, strictly before the frame count
   `quantizeToFrames` (`nsf.ts`) uses to decide when to wrap PLAY back to
   its own loop frame; past that point the exported player has already
   looped, so GME's trace there holds the *loop frame's* content, not the
   tail source frame's, and comparing the two would fail for a reason that
   has nothing to do with export fidelity. This can only ever exclude
   frames within a few of the very end of a capture, and only on a file
   whose last written frame lands that close to its own loop point - on this
   corpus that is `zelda-native` (1 frame), `zelda-rendition` (2 frames) and
   `pently-demo` (1 frame), reported on the sheet as "excluding N frame(s)
   past the loop wrap" - and every file's comparable frames, on all twelve,
   now match exactly. This proof is not audio, so it cannot be fooled by a
   mixer curve or an alignment choice; together with proof #1 it is what
   makes proof #3's threshold trustworthy. A cheap negative check
   (`packages/chipvoice/test/nsf.mjs`, no GME needed - it drives the same
   `compareFrameWrites` this proof uses against this project's own offline
   replay of a real export) corrupts one write and asserts the gate reports
   it as exactly one mismatched frame, so the gate's bite is itself tested,
   not just its pass case.
3. Export loss (the coarse secondary gate, on top of proofs #1 and #2's
   exact ones): GME's own trace of playing the export,
   parsed back into register writes and rendered through this project's own
   `renderPerformance`, against a render of the *source* capture's untouched
   events through that same `renderPerformance`. Both sides go through the
   identical 2A03 DSP, so the only thing left to differ is what the export
   itself changed. Given proof #2, that is not missing or wrong commands -
   it is *when within a frame* a write lands: the exported player applies a
   whole frame's writes back to back within a few hundred cycles of PLAY
   starting, while a source driver spends its own, longer PLAY routine on
   other work first and only reaches a given channel's registers well into
   the frame, so a note-on a driver reaches partway through its frame plays
   for more of that one frame in the export than it did in the source. The
   metric is relative RMS error between the two renders' per-NTSC-frame
   loudness envelopes (one RMS value per 735-sample frame at 44.1 kHz, this
   format's own replay granularity, not a raw sample-domain difference,
   which a phase-sensitive pulse/noise wave would fail even when it sounds
   identical), each side peak-normalized on its own, with the constant frame
   offset that minimizes the error searched for (±8 frames around each
   side's own first-sound guess) rather than assumed from that guess alone -
   an earlier version of this metric skipped that search, so the two sides'
   independently chosen onsets routinely landed one frame apart, and on
   percussive content that one frame of slip alone measured 13.3-50.7%,
   written up at the time as "quantization cost" from effects updating
   faster than 60 Hz - a claim that does not survive contact with the files
   (every one of them plays at 16666-16639 microseconds per PLAY call per
   its own NSF header, i.e. already once per 60 Hz frame). With the search
   in place, measured on this corpus: 4.5-8.9% on Mario/Zelda, and
   4.5-21.9% across all twelve files. On `after-the-rain` (21.9%, this
   corpus's highest), the pulse-1 envelope restart for its second frame's
   note lands at cycle 6718 of the source's own ~29780-cycle frame (about
   23% in) but at cycle 546 of the export's corresponding frame (under 2%
   in) - the shift a note-on's timing takes within one frame, exactly the
   mechanism above, not a wrong or missing command (proof #2 already rules
   that out for this file). The stated threshold (below) sits a real margin
   above that whole corrected measured band; a wrong note, a dropped channel
   or a loop gone wrong - which collapse a section's envelope to silence or
   to a different shape across many frames at once, not shift one attack
   frame's energy within a single 16.7 ms window - would clear it by a wide
   margin, the way the pre-search version of this metric did (well over
   100%).
4. GME mixer comparison (informational, not a gate): GME's rendered PCM of
   the export against this project's own render of the *source* (same
   envelope metric as above). This crosses two independently written 2A03
   emulators, so a sizeable residual is expected even for a perfect export -
   GME's DAC/mixer curve and this project's own do not level a note to the
   same loudness - and it says nothing about which side is "right"; every
   exportable file in this corpus matches GME's command stream exactly
   (proof #1), matches it frame for frame (proof #2, 100%) and clears the
   export-loss gate (proof #3) yet still measures 21.3-51.6% here. Reported
   on the sheet for visibility, never gating CI.

## Driver coverage

What `packages/chipvoice/src/driver.ts` writes when it plays a note. The core
implements more than this; the rest is written from the wiki and exercised by
nothing chipvoice plays.

| Voice | Exercised | Not exercised |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pulse 1, 2 | `$4000` with duty, halt and a constant volume, rewritten when it changes; `$4001 = $08`, sweep off with negate set; `$4002`; `$4003` with length 31 at note start and when the period high bits jump by more than one, which restarts the phase; a high-bit change of one goes through the sweep unit instead - low byte to `$FF` or `$00`, `$4001 = $87` or `$8F`, a forced half-frame clock by `$4017 = $C0`, then `$4001 = $08` and the low byte - so a vibrato or a slide does not reset the phase; silence is `$4000 = $30` | envelope decay; length counter expiry; the sweep unit counting on its own clock |
| Triangle | `$4008 = $FF`, control flag with linear 127; `$400A`; `$400B` with length 31; silence is `$4008 = $00` then `$400B`, so the linear counter reloads with 0 | length or linear counter expiry by counting; the ultrasonic mute below period 2, which is implemented |

| `$4015` | written once at power-on, `$0F` | clearing a bit mid-song |

## Known deviations

| What | Deliberate | Why | Affects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The DMC's DMA does not stall a CPU, and its IRQ is a flag nothing reads | yes | there is no CPU | none for a driver; a test ROM that counts DMA cycles |
| The DMC's output unit powers on silent with eight bits remaining, as nesdev describes | no | Mesen agrees (eight); Nes_Snd_Emu does not (one) | the first sample byte's start, by up to seven bit periods against Nes_Snd_Emu; against Mesen the bit count agrees but the first byte still lands 54 cycles apart, since neither oracle simulates the CPU stall a real DMA read causes |
| The frame IRQ is not implemented, and `$4015` cannot be read | yes | there is no CPU to interrupt or to read | `apu_test` cases that read the status register |
| `$4014` and `$4016` are ignored, and writes are decoded without the CPU's bus timing | yes | no CPU | none for a driver; a cycle-level oracle comparison may show a one-cycle offset on the write itself, and a period write on the cycle a pulse reloads is seen by that reload, where Mesen reloads first (see the second-oracle section) |
| Resampling is a box filter per output sample rather than band-limited step synthesis | partly | simple, fast, and the grit is wanted | aliasing on high pulse notes. Parity is measured before it; the analog sheet reports it |
| Output filters are first-order sections at the approximate corners, with no unit named, and a fixed gain of 2.9 | no | no measurement yet | the analog sheet |
| The envelope and the sweep are implemented from the wiki; the test ROMs do not reach them | no | Mesen verifies both (2026-09-27, P2-1): the envelope's levels and the sweep's arithmetic and divider timing match; what differs is Mesen's power-on sweep state and when it refreshes an output, both on the oracle's side (see the second-oracle section) | none known |

## Power-on state

`reset()` is power-on: every unit fresh, `$4015` and `$4017` at zero, nothing
queued. The cycle clock and the filters are left alone - the sample clock did not
stop, and zeroing a filter is a step through it. Power-on: every voice disabled
until `$4015` is written, the LFSR at 1 (as the hardware), the frame counter at
cycle 0 of the 4-step sequence, the triangle at step 0 (which outputs 15, as the hardware), every timer at 0, the DMC's
level at 0 with eight silent bits to go. Memory is not touched by a reset; a
cartridge is not. The output stage settles its filters on the first sample, so
the triangle's power-on 15 is not a step through them.

## History

Newest first.

- 2026-09-27 · Every divergence against Mesen is traced (P2-1). The sweep's
  is Mesen's power-on divider and period, both 0 and outside its own range,
  not its algorithm; the envelope's and the noise's residuals are Mesen
  refreshing an output only on a write or a timer tick; the songs' pulses
  differ on a write landing on a reload's own cycle, a convention, and on the
  `$4017` delay's parity, which was the shim's and is fixed there, lifting
  `song-e2e`'s pulse 2 to 100 % and the corpus from 65.47 % to 65.81 %. No
  core change; no golden hash moved.
- 2026-09-27 · A second oracle, Mesen 2, checks the envelope, the sweep and
  the noise's bit pattern - none of which the 2005 oracle or the test ROMs
  settle. The envelope and the triangle's phase agree once running; the
  sweep's target-period arithmetic agrees but its divider's timing does not,
  favoring this core's reading of nesdev's page over Mesen's, per the
  second-oracle section above. No core change; no golden hash moved.
- 2026-09-04 · Smooth vibrato. A pulse's period high bits move by one through
  the sweep unit and a forced `$4017` clock, blargg's technique as FamiStudio
  writes it, instead of through `$4003`, which restarts the phase. The golden
  hash moved: every vibrato across a period boundary lost its click. The
  oracle clocks the forced half frame three cycles early, and one note on
  pulse 2 of the e2e song reloaded its timer inside those cycles; the
  baseline says so (`8220846152b9937b`).
- 2026-09-04 · The triangle powers on at step 0, output 15, as the hardware.
  Blargg's mixer test walks it from power-on to the sequence's zero and leaves
  it there; from the previous start it landed on 15 and detuned the whole
  mixing table, and the measurement said so. The output stage settles its
  filters on the first sample instead. Golden hash moved.
- 2026-09-04 · The frame IRQ flag, `$4015` read back, the reset button, and
  two things only the hardware knew: a halt flag written on a length clock's
  cycle takes effect after it, and a length reload on that cycle is ignored
  unless the counter was zero. Every one of blargg's APU test ROMs passes on a
  6502 the harness carries. Golden hash unchanged.
- 2026-09-04 · The DMC exists: a 7-bit level nudged by bits read from memory,
  at the rate table's pace, with `$4011` direct writes, looping, and `$4015`
  start and stop. Memory comes in through `load`, on the core, the worklet and
  the harness's logs. No instrument reaches it yet.
- 2026-09-04 · Register writes are bytes on `$4000-$4017`, decoded the way the
  chip decodes them. Two things the driver could do before and the hardware
  cannot are gone: a pulse's period high bits now change only through `$4003`,
  which restarts the phase, and `$4001` is written with `$08`, so pulse notes at
  period `$400` and above - G#2 and below - are no longer muted by the sweep
  unit. The 5-step frame sequence and the `$4017` write delay exist. Golden hash
  moved.
- 2026-09-04 · A write lands on the CPU cycle it is stamped with instead of at
  the start of the sample around it. Golden hash moved by the sub-sample shift.
- 2026-09-04 · The triangle holds its last value at note off instead of dropping
  to zero. Click on every bass note gone.
- 2026-09-04 · Half frames on the second and fourth steps of the sequence, not
  the first and third. Inaudible with the shipped driver.
- 2026-09-04 · The noise channel ran at half the hardware rate: the period table
  is in CPU cycles and the timer counted at the APU rate. Every drum was an
  octave dark. Golden hash changed with it.
